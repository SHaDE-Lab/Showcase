One question, three ways to answer it

How hot does a place feel to a person standing in it? Mean radiant temperature (MRT) captures the radiation a body absorbs from sun, sky and surfaces, and it often drives outdoor thermal stress more than air temperature does. Each tool below estimates it from a different kind of data.

Project 01 · Routing

Cool Routes

Real-time human thermal exposure routing. Cool Routes finds walking paths across the ASU campus that minimize radiant heat load and compares them side by side with the shortest path.

  • Hourly MRT forecasts. SOLWEIG is run on LiDAR-derived surface models, with the next 72 hours buffered so routes are instant.
  • Heat-weighted network. MRT is sampled along every segment of the OpenStreetMap walking graph and used as an edge cost.
  • Cool vs. shortest. Every query returns both routes with distance and mean MRT, plus a map overlay of the MRT raster and KML export.
  • Production stack. React + ArcGIS map front end, a Flask routing API, and Dockerized hourly and daily SOLWEIG jobs.

Project 02 · Computer vision + physics

VisionMRT

Upload a 360° photo, or pick a street-level panorama from a map, and VisionMRT reconstructs the scene, casts shadows through the day and solves a surface energy balance to give MRT, UTCI and PET.

  • From imagery to physics. Transformer-based segmentation and monocular depth estimation turn each cube face into surfaces with class and distance.
  • Sky view factor, done properly. Computed as the cosine-weighted share of open sky from all six faces; unit tests check it against the analytic sin²θ cone.
  • Explore the result. Cube-map, panorama, hemisphere and interactive 3D views, plus a segmentation editor with marker, magic-wand and tree-brush tools.
  • Validated. Reported accuracy against 1,174 observations at 132 Phoenix-area sites: R² 0.94, RMSE 3.12 °C, MAE 2.61 °C (Sustainable Cities and Society, 2026).
  • Scales to many users. Separate web, CPU and GPU workers with job queues, tested for concurrency, oversized uploads and worker failure.

Project 03 · Machine learning

WebMRT

An online tool that predicts summertime mean radiant temperature from routinely available meteorological data, built for urban planners and climate decision-makers who need an answer without running a full radiation model.

  • Trained on field measurements. Models learn from MaRTy, the lab’s mobile six-direction instrument platform, across hot-arid summer conditions.
  • Fast and accessible. Enter weather conditions in the browser and get an MRT estimate in seconds, with no modeling background required.
  • Benchmarked. Several algorithms were compared; LightGBM performed best (R² = 0.92 on held-out data).
  • Peer-reviewed. Published in Sustainable Cities and Society (2024).

Tool 01 · Mobile station

MaRTy

A mobile human-biometeorological station that rolls through a place and measures what a pedestrian actually feels. Every two seconds it records air temperature, humidity, wind, position and radiation from six directions, and turns them into mean radiant temperature.

  • Six-directional radiation. Three four-component net radiometers, twelve sensors in all, measure shortwave and longwave radiation from above, below and the four compass directions at about 1.1 to 1.3 m, around torso height.
  • Weather at pedestrian height. A Rotronic HygroClip2 probe for air temperature and humidity, a Gill two-dimensional WindSonic anemometer and a Garmin GPS.
  • From fluxes to MRT. The six radiant fluxes are combined with angular factors for a standing person (0.06 for up and down, 0.22 for each horizontal direction) to give mean radiant temperature.
  • Proven in the field. First used for hourly transects at 22 campus sites on 19 June 2016, the record 47.8 °C day in Tempe. Its measurements are the training data behind WebMRT.

Tool 02 · Low-cost sensor

MaRTiny

MaRTy’s tiny sibling: a low-cost IoT biometeorological device that estimates mean radiant temperature from a globe thermometer, and uses embedded computer vision to count how many people stand in sun and in shade.

  • Globe and weather sensing. A grey globe on a DS18B20 probe, an air probe under a white radiation cup, a DHT22 humidity sensor, a UV sensor and a cup anemometer, all read by an Arduino Uno.
  • Computer vision. An ESP32-CAM captures images of the space; a server runs YOLO pedestrian detection and shadow segmentation on them to count how many people stand in sun and in shade.
  • Solar powered. A south-facing solar panel feeds a battery in a second box on the lamp post, which also holds a Wi-Fi dongle and powers the sensor box. A machine-learning correction brings globe-based MRT to an RMSE of about 4 °C against the six-directional reference, as a proof of concept (2022 paper).
  • In the parks. Mounted on a lamp post, with the sensor box on one side and a south-facing solar panel, carrying the globe thermometer and UV sensor, on the other. Deployed in City of Tempe parks from 2021 to 2023 to study how the use of public space changes with heat.
